**Brentwood** remains one of the most consistently competitive custom home markets on the Los Angeles Westside — driven by land value, school access, and proximity that has not meaningfully changed in decades. For many buyers and long-time owners, the real question is not whether to invest in Brentwood, but whether to renovate an existing structure or build custom from the lot up.

Our [luxury home building](/services/luxury-home-builder-los-angeles) team works this market regularly, and the lot-first evaluation we apply everywhere matters especially here, where land value drives so much of the decision.

## Why Land Value Changes the Renovate-vs-Build Math

In many markets, renovating an existing home is the default lower-cost path. In Brentwood, that assumption needs to be tested, not assumed.

### The lot is often worth more than the structure

Given current land values, many older Brentwood homes carry structures that are worth significantly less than the land beneath them. That reality shifts the calculus toward a teardown-rebuild path more often than in less land-constrained markets. This is especially true for homes built in the postwar decades, where a modest one-story ranch or traditional sits on a lot whose value is driven almost entirely by location rather than the structure itself.

### Renovation cost can approach new construction cost

Bringing an older Brentwood home fully up to current standards — systems, structure, and finishes — can approach the cost of new construction once the full scope is understood, which is exactly why this evaluation needs real numbers, not assumptions, before a direction is chosen. Once a renovation starts requiring foundation work, a full electrical and plumbing replacement, and a new roof structure, the marginal savings over a teardown-rebuild often narrow considerably, while the finished result under a rebuild carries no legacy conditions.

## What Actually Constrains a Brentwood Custom Home Design

Owner vision matters, but the lot itself sets real limits.

### Lot size and shape drive the floor plan

Brentwood lots vary meaningfully in size and shape, and the buildable envelope on a given parcel — after setbacks, easements, and any specific plan requirements — often determines the realistic scale of the home before a single design decision is made. Corner lots and irregularly shaped parcels in particular can carry setback requirements on more than two sides, which meaningfully shrinks the usable building envelope compared to a standard rectangular interior lot of the same total square footage.

### Neighborhood-specific considerations add another layer

Some areas of Brentwood carry additional design review or specific plan requirements beyond standard citywide code, and certain hillside-adjacent pockets north of Sunset carry the same Baseline Hillside Ordinance considerations as Bel Air or the Pacific Palisades. Understanding whether a given lot falls under any of these considerations early avoids redesign later in the process.

### FAR and R1 zoning shape the ceiling on size

Most of Brentwood is zoned R1, which ties maximum floor area to lot size through the citywide floor area ratio formula, with adjustments for garage space, porches, and other exemptions. Owners often arrive with a target square footage in mind based on comparable homes they've toured, without realizing that FAR calculations — not aesthetic preference — set the real ceiling on what a given lot can support.

## Cost Per Square Foot Realities on the Westside

Custom home construction costs in Brentwood and the broader Westside sit meaningfully above citywide averages, driven by labor availability, finish expectations, and the complexity that comes with tight infill lots, shared property lines, and the logistics of building in a dense residential neighborhood. Site work, foundation systems appropriate to the specific soil conditions, and the systems infrastructure expected in a genuinely custom home — advanced HVAC zoning, structured wiring, home automation infrastructure — all factor into a realistic budget well beyond basic shell construction costs.

## What the Permitting and Construction Process Looks Like

A full custom home in this market moves through design, engineering, permitting, and construction — with each phase shaped by the specific lot.

### Design and engineering set the foundation

Architectural design, structural engineering, and any required geotechnical work need to be substantially resolved before permit submission, since incomplete design packages are the most common source of extended plan check timelines. On a typical Brentwood custom home, plan check alone often runs several months from initial submission through final approval, with the timeline extending further if the project requires a variance, a zoning adjustment, or design review sign-off.

### Demolition permitting comes first on a teardown

Before new construction plan check can proceed, a demolition permit is required for the existing structure, and LADBS requires specific notices and, in some cases, asbestos survey documentation before demolition can begin — a step owners sometimes overlook when estimating how quickly a project can move from purchase to groundbreaking.

### Construction sequencing follows a full custom home pattern

Once permitted, a Brentwood custom home proceeds through site work, foundation, framing, systems, and finishes in a sequence similar to any ground-up luxury home — with the specific site conditions of the lot shaping pace along the way. Tight infill lots with limited staging area often require more careful sequencing of material deliveries and trade scheduling than a comparable project on a larger parcel, since there's simply less room on-site to store materials or stage equipment.

## Working With Architects and Engineers Who Know Brentwood

Because so much of what can be built on a Brentwood lot depends on the specific interaction between FAR, setbacks, and any neighborhood-specific overlay, an architect and structural engineer with direct experience in this exact market will identify constraints during initial feasibility rather than midway through design. This matters more here than in less land-constrained markets, since a design that has to be substantially reworked after a City Planning consultation costs real time and architectural fees that a more experienced team would likely have avoided from the outset. When evaluating a design team for a Brentwood project, ask specifically about their track record with FAR-constrained infill lots in this exact submarket, not just general custom home experience elsewhere in Los Angeles.

## Financing and Timeline Considerations for a Ground-Up Build

Construction loans for ground-up custom homes are underwritten differently than renovation financing, typically requiring a completed architectural and engineering package, a fixed construction budget, and a draw schedule tied to inspection milestones. Owners should engage their lender early in the design process, since the lender's appraisal and underwriting requirements can influence design and specification decisions well before construction begins. Building the full realistic timeline — lot acquisition or demolition, design, permitting, and construction — into a financing plan from the start avoids the common mismatch between loan term assumptions and how long a genuinely custom Brentwood home actually takes to deliver.

A construction loan lender will also typically require a licensed, bonded, and adequately insured CSLB contractor of record before releasing initial draws, and will scrutinize the contractor's track record on projects of comparable size — another reason the contractor selection and financing conversations should happen in parallel rather than sequentially. Builder's risk insurance, covering the structure during construction against fire, weather, and other perils, is a separate policy owners need to arrange before groundbreaking, and it should be coordinated with the lender's requirements from the outset rather than treated as paperwork to resolve later.
