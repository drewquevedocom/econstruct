**Encino, Sherman Oaks, and Studio City** have quietly become one of the more active luxury remodel markets in Los Angeles — not because they are new, but because homeowners here are increasingly choosing to invest in place rather than sell and move to the Westside.

Our [luxury modernization](/services/luxury-modernization) team has worked across this stretch of the San Fernando Valley for years, and the pattern is consistent: larger lots and strong home values are giving owners real room to expand and upgrade.

## What Makes This Market Different From the Westside

The single biggest structural difference between the Valley and the Westside is lot size — and it changes what is actually possible on a given property.

### More room to build

Encino, Sherman Oaks, and Studio City lots are, on average, larger than comparable Westside parcels. That extra room supports additions, ADUs, pools, and outdoor living spaces that would be difficult to fit on a smaller Beverly Hills or Brentwood lot without significant compromise. A number of properties south of Ventura Boulevard, in particular, sit on quarter-acre-plus lots that leave real room for a detached ADU and a pool without crowding the primary residence — something increasingly rare on comparable Westside parcels at similar price points.

### Older housing stock means more systems work

Many homes in this corridor were built decades ago, in a mid-century building boom that produced solid framing but systems that have long since aged past their service life. Electrical panels, plumbing, and structural framing were often designed for a different era of use, which means a modernization project here frequently includes systems upgrades that a newer property might not need. It's common to find original cast-iron drain lines, undersized 100-amp electrical service, and single-pane windows on homes that are otherwise excellent candidates for a high-end remodel — the bones are good, but the systems need a full refresh to support a genuinely luxury result.

### Zoning still varies block by block

Despite the Valley's reputation for larger lots, zoning is not uniform — R1 single-family zones sit alongside pockets of R2 and hillside-designated parcels, and FAR, height, and setback rules shift accordingly. Some Encino and Sherman Oaks neighborhoods also carry Neighborhood Conservation or specific plan overlays intended to preserve architectural character, which can affect what a remodel or addition is permitted to look like from the street.

## Where the Hillside Rules Still Apply

Not all of this market is flat. The northern edges of Encino and Studio City rise into hillside terrain with the same regulatory profile as other LA hillside neighborhoods.

### Geotechnical requirements do not disappear in the Valley

Hillside lots in this area require the same soils and geotechnical evaluation as hillside lots in Bel Air or the Hollywood Hills. Owners on sloped Valley parcels should not assume flat-lot rules apply. We've seen owners assume a hillside lot in Encino is a "flat lot with a view" simply because it's in the Valley, only to discover during design that the same Baseline Hillside Ordinance and grading review that applies in Bel Air applies here too.

### Grading and setback limits still shape design

Valley hillside properties are subject to the same grading quantity and height limitations that apply citywide, which affects how large an addition or new structure can realistically be. Retaining wall height limits, haul route approval, and slope-based floor area calculations all apply identically to a hillside lot in Studio City as they would in the Hollywood Hills.

## What Homeowners Are Actually Building

The projects we see most often in this corridor tell a clear story about how families are using their investment.

### Whole-home modernization over teardown

Given the strength of the existing lots and, in many cases, sound foundations, whole-home modernization is frequently the more efficient path compared to a full rebuild — though that determination still requires a structural assessment case by case. Ranch-style and mid-century homes in this area, in particular, often have foundations and framing that hold up well to engineering review, making a full-gut modernization that keeps the shell and rebuilds everything inside a genuinely attractive alternative to demolition.

### Outdoor living and ADUs

With more usable lot area than the Westside typically offers, pools, outdoor kitchens, and detached ADUs are consistently among the most requested additions in this market. The larger average lot also means detached ADUs here can often be sited away from the primary residence entirely, which simplifies utility routing and gives the ADU genuine privacy — something much harder to achieve on a tighter Westside parcel.

## Working With a Contractor Who Knows Both Markets

Because this corridor bridges Valley-scale lots with Westside-level finish expectations, the contractor matters. A team experienced only in tight urban infill projects may not have the systems-upgrade expertise older Valley homes require, while a team unfamiliar with luxury finish standards may undersell what these larger lots can support. We approach Encino, Sherman Oaks, and Studio City projects with the same finish-level standards we apply on the Westside, adjusted for the systems realities of older Valley housing stock.

## Permitting and Cost Realities in This Market

Plan check for a significant remodel or addition in the Valley moves through the same LADBS process as anywhere else in the city, and timelines are comparable — a well-prepared submission for a whole-home modernization or major addition commonly runs a few months through first review, longer if the scope includes a second story, a pool, or hillside grading. Cost per square foot for a genuinely high-end remodel in this corridor tracks closely with Westside pricing once finish level, systems scope, and structural work are accounted for; the primary savings in this market tend to come from land cost rather than construction cost, which is part of why owners here often choose to invest more heavily in the build itself.

### Older systems infrastructure affects sequencing

Because so much of the housing stock here predates modern electrical and plumbing standards, a full modernization frequently needs to sequence a panel upgrade, a full re-pipe, and structural seismic improvements alongside the visible remodel work — all of which benefit from being planned together rather than treated as separate projects layered on top of each other over several years. Doing this work in one coordinated phase, while walls and floors are already open for the remodel, is consistently more cost-effective than addressing each system separately over time.
