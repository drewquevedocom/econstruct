One of the earliest decisions in any significant Los Angeles construction project is not about design at all — it is about **project delivery**. Should the architect and builder be one coordinated team, or should the owner hire an independent architect and contractor separately? Both models produce excellent homes. They allocate risk, communication, and accountability in genuinely different ways.

As a firm that offers [custom home construction](/services/custom-home-construction-los-angeles) under both delivery models, we think owners deserve a clear-eyed comparison rather than a pitch for whichever model a given firm happens to offer.

## How Design-Build Actually Works

In a design-build project, the architectural design and construction responsibility sit under one team, working together from the start.

### Cost feedback happens earlier

Because the builder is involved from early design, cost implications of design decisions surface in real time — rather than after a full drawing set is complete and sent out for competitive pricing. This tends to produce more reliable budget alignment earlier in the process. It also means value-engineering conversations — swapping a structural approach or a material to hit budget — happen while the design is still flexible, rather than after construction drawings are finished and the owner is emotionally and financially invested in a specific design.

### Accountability is consolidated, for better and worse

With one team responsible for both design and construction, there is no gap for design and construction responsibility to fall between two separate parties. The tradeoff is that the owner needs to evaluate that single team's design capability directly, since there is no independent design check built into the structure. When a change order or a field conflict arises under design-build, there's no ambiguity about who resolves it — the same team that designed the detail is responsible for building it, which removes a common source of finger-pointing on separately delivered projects.

### Permitting can move more efficiently

Because the design-build team controls both the drawings and the construction sequencing, they can often structure permit submissions — phased permits for grading, foundation, and superstructure, for example — around actual construction logistics rather than a purely design-driven document set. This coordination can shave real time off a project's overall schedule on complex Los Angeles projects where plan check happens in stages.

## How the Traditional Architect-Then-Contractor Model Works

In this model, the owner hires an architect independently, completes design, and then selects a contractor — either through negotiation or competitive bid — to build it.

### The architect works exclusively for the owner

An independently retained architect has no financial relationship with the builder, which some owners value as an independent voice through design and into construction administration. This includes reviewing contractor pay applications, evaluating change order requests, and acting as the owner's technical advocate if a dispute arises during construction — a role that doesn't exist in the same form under design-build.

### Coordination becomes the owner's responsibility

Without a single unified team, the owner (often with the architect's help) needs to actively manage the handoff between design and construction, including resolving any gaps or conflicts that surface once a contractor begins pricing and building the design. A common friction point is the moment a general contractor prices a completed architectural set and identifies items that were underestimated or unbuildable as drawn — under the separate model, resolving that gap involves the owner mediating between two parties who don't work for the same firm.

### Competitive bidding has real tradeoffs

One argument for the separate model is the ability to competitively bid a completed design among multiple general contractors. This can surface real pricing variance, but it also means the design has to be essentially complete before real cost feedback exists — a risk if the design turns out to exceed budget significantly once priced, since redesign at that stage costs both time and architectural fees that a design-build approach would have caught earlier.

## How to Actually Decide

The right model depends less on which is theoretically better and more on the specific team and project in front of you.

### Evaluate the design-build team's actual design portfolio

If considering design-build, look closely at the design quality of that specific team's past work — not just their construction credentials. Design-build only delivers on its promise when the design side is genuinely strong. Ask to see completed projects in person if possible, not just photographs, and ask specifically who on the team held the architectural license of record for those projects.

### Consider how involved you want to be as owner

The separate-architect model generally requires more active owner coordination between two parties. Design-build requires more owner diligence up front in selecting the right team, since so much accountability sits in one place. Owners who travel frequently or manage multiple properties often prefer design-build specifically because it reduces the number of parties they need to actively manage during construction.

### Contract structure matters regardless of model

Whichever model you choose, the contract terms matter as much as the delivery model itself. A design-build agreement should clearly define the design development process, when pricing becomes fixed, and how changes are handled; a separate-architect arrangement should clearly define the architect's construction administration role and how disputes between architect and contractor get resolved. A well-structured contract under either model prevents most of the disputes that give either delivery method a bad reputation.

## Hybrid Approaches Worth Considering

Some projects benefit from a middle path: an owner retains an independent architect for schematic design and concept development, then brings on a design-build team to complete construction documents and build the project, with the original architect staying involved in a reduced capacity for design review. This can preserve some of the independent-advocate benefit of the separate model while gaining design-build's cost feedback and construction coordination advantages during the phases where they matter most. It requires clear agreement up front about roles and fees, since a poorly defined hybrid arrangement can create exactly the coordination gaps both pure models are trying to avoid.

## Questions to Ask Before Committing to Either Model

Regardless of which delivery model you're leaning toward, ask any team you're evaluating how they handle scope changes, how pricing is communicated as design develops, and who has final authority over material and structural decisions once construction begins. Ask for references specifically from projects of similar scope and complexity to yours, and ask those references directly about how disputes or unexpected conditions were handled — that answer tells you more about how the team will perform under real project pressure than any portfolio photo can.

Contract structure and payment terms deserve the same scrutiny under either model — a fixed-price contract, a cost-plus arrangement with a guaranteed maximum price, and open-book cost-plus billing all allocate financial risk differently between owner and builder, and the right structure often depends on how well-defined the scope is before construction begins. Owners should also confirm that whichever contract form is used includes clear provisions for how allowances, unforeseen conditions, and design changes are priced and approved, since ambiguity in these clauses is where most delivery-model disputes actually originate, regardless of whether the project is design-build or separately delivered.
